# Rounds 4 and 6 with AWS in the data path (v1.1)

Status: **approved for Round 4 (2026-09-28) and Round 6 (2026-09-29), and both are built on branch
`v1.1` from main. Round 6's AWS lane is DMS plus Glue into Delta, measured through the built engine
below. The release bar is next.** § 11 records the decisions.

Protocols: `round4-two-lane-v1` and `round6-two-lane-v1`. The branch reserved
`round4-establish-score-delivery-v2` and `round6-establish-checkout-ingestion-v2` for a
cold-setup contest, with its own contract: a commit after readiness, signed receipts, a promotion
ledger. This design does not adopt that contract, so it does not reuse the IDs.

This is the design of record for v1.1. It supersedes `competitive-rounds-v2.md`,
`resource-contract.md`, `receipt-and-claims.md`, `ROUND4_REFERENCE_WRITER.md` and
`ROUND6_LAKEFLOW_CONNECT.md`. Those files exist only on `fix/contest-ledger-set-integrity`, where they
stay as the record of the contest explored there, and as input. Every rule kept from them is restated
here where it is used, so this document stands on main without them.

## Owner requirement

- **Run the AWS stack instead of describing it.** Rounds 4 and 6 get a real, timed AWS lane beside
  Lakebase, against both Aurora Serverless v2 and RDS PostgreSQL.
- **Consistent with the other rounds.** Every round times its own task, end to end, from the same
  starting state on both lanes.
  - Round 2's task needs an isolated copy of the database, so building that copy is on its clock.
    The same holds for Round 3's restore and Round 5's pooled path.
  - Rounds 4 and 6 are named for **moving data**. In production nobody rebuilds a sync pipeline, a
    writer job or a change-capture task for each change. These integrations are standing, just as every
    round's databases are standing and nobody times creating them.
  - So the clock is the time to move the data: 8–10 seconds for Lakebase in Round 4 today. That
    clock is what made the original Round 4 worth watching.
- **Simple enough to be stable.** Round 5 was a beast to stabilize, and Rounds 4 and 6 must not
  repeat it.
- **Prepare must not make the room wait.** A cold Round 4 Prepare takes a minute today.
- **The same bar as v1.0.0.** v1.1 ships only after passing [docs/RELEASING.md](../RELEASING.md)
  and `scripts/release_bar/run.sh`, extended so the bar exercises the new lanes rather than passing
  around them (see [Release bar](#9-release-bar-for-v11)).
- **Nothing done by hand.** A virgin `./bootstrap.sh --apply --deploy-app --yes` produces both lanes
  with nothing done on the side.

## The shape in one paragraph

Each round becomes v1's proven one-lane round, run twice.

- **Both integrations are standing.** The installer builds the Lakebase one and the AWS one, and AWS
  moves the data in both rounds. Round 4's AWS lane is an AWS Glue streaming job writing into Aurora
  or RDS. Round 6's is AWS DMS change capture from Aurora or RDS into S3, and an AWS Glue job that
  appends those changes to a Delta table in the lakehouse: Round 4's writer in reverse.
- **Cold at the bell.** In Round 4 both lanes are parked when the bell rings (Ryan, 2026-09-28), and
  the card says "both integrations cold start at the bell". In Round 6 the AWS lane is parked, and
  Lakebase's lane is its built-in change feed, which has no start or stop to park (Ryan chose this,
  2026-09-29, § 2). The card says so, and What It Cost prices keeping AWS's pipeline running.
- **Prepare** starts nothing. It confirms every parkable lane is parked and the baseline is exact, so
  it takes seconds.
- **The bell** starts the parked integrations at the same moment and makes the change. Round 4 uses
  one Delta commit that both lanes read. Round 6 commits the same checkout to both sources.
- **Each lane's clock** runs from the change to its own first exact read. A parked lane's clock
  contains its integration's start.
- **What a bout leaves behind:** only rows, and those are restored or deleted. Round 6's DMS
  replication slot is standing, like its task.
- **No journal.** Every object has a deterministic name, so provider state is the ledger.

**Two clocks, and only one is the service's.** Reverse ETL sync is the synced table's own
timestamps; nothing the app does can move it. The Full proof time ends at the app's read, so it
includes the app's own sync-status polling. On v1.0.0 each poll was a full inspection (nine
control-plane GETs, then a `DESCRIBE HISTORY` of about 1.3 s) that added 3–6 s. PR #41 cut that to
about 1.5 s. This matters for fairness in § 3: the AWS writer has no service timestamp like
Reverse ETL sync, so the scored clock must be read the same way on both lanes.

**The wait that is left after the bell** is mostly the Delta `MERGE` on the SQL warehouse, about
3.2 s median. It lands before the commit timestamp, so it is in neither clock, and both lanes share it
in v1.1 because they share the commit.

### A live defect the run found (fixed in PR #41)

Round 4's re-do failed in two of three bouts with `SQLSTATE 2D521`, a Delta concurrent-transaction
conflict.

- **The cause.** `_finish_model_score` (`server/manager.py`) publishes the verified result with
  **Re-do READY**, and in the same step schedules `settle_and_restore_baseline`. That setting
  restores the source row with a Delta `MERGE`.
- **The race.** A re-do pressed within the next ~15 s sends its own `MERGE` into the same row, and
  Delta rejects whichever commits second. When the re-do wins (bout 1), the restore lands on top of
  its new score.
- **The evidence.** The table's Delta history holds only the app's own one-row `MERGE`s
  (v186–v195).
- **Why v1.0.0's 114/114 missed it.** The release bar never presses Round 4's re-do; `chaos.py` only
  POSTs `/cooldown`.
- **The fix, shipped in PR #41.**
  - The re-do and the next arm wait for the previous bout's restore, and the restore is scheduled
    before the result is published.
  - A Delta concurrent-transaction conflict is retried with a bound.
  - Still owed to the bar: a finish-then-re-do scenario (§ 9).

### Product facts this design depends on (vendor documentation, read 2026-09-27)

- **Aurora cannot pause with logical replication on.** In an Aurora PostgreSQL cluster with logical
  replication enabled, the writer and the tier-0 and tier-1 readers do not auto-pause.
- **Lakeflow Connect's PostgreSQL connector** is in **Public Preview** and needs account-team
  enablement. No longer a dependency: it would put Databricks in the AWS lane, and Round 6's AWS lane
  is DMS plus Glue (2026-09-29, § 4). Two of its facts still hold for any logical-replication
  consumer:
  - its ingestion gateway runs on classic Job Compute, needs at least 8 cores, and runs
    continuously;
  - deleting an ingestion pipeline does not drop its replication slot; that is manual;
  - leaving `max_slot_wal_keep_size` at `-1` is discouraged.
- **AWS's routes into a lakehouse table, checked 2026-09-29.** DMS's S3 target writes CSV or Parquet
  files only. Aurora and RDS zero-ETL target Redshift only. Glue zero-ETL takes no Aurora or RDS
  source. Firehose's database source writes Iceberg tables, but is in preview.
- **Synced tables.** Continuous mode needs change data feed on the source Delta table and runs on a
  managed Lakeflow pipeline.
- **Serverless streaming triggers.** Serverless compute supports only `Trigger.AvailableNow` for
  streaming.
- **Pipeline sinks.** Lakeflow pipelines offer `@dp.foreach_batch_sink()`. Its documentation page
  does not state its maturity, or whether it runs serverless in continuous mode.

## Decisions

### 1. What is timed

- **The score, per lane.** From the bell, which starts both lanes' integrations from parked and
  commits the change, to the completion of the first read that returns the exact expected outcome.
  Both lanes share that one start instant, on the controller's clock.
- **Round 4: one change, two destinations.** The bell commits one Delta `MERGE` on one shared source
  table. The synced table and the AWS writer both read that commit, so there is no launch skew.
- **Round 6: two sources, one moment.** Two databases need two commits, so the bell commits the same
  checkout to both concurrently.
  - Each lane's clock starts at its own commit's acknowledgment.
  - The launch skew is recorded, and a bout whose skew exceeds 100 ms is not scorable.
- **No re-do.** Round 6 has none, as in v1. Round 4 drops its re-do (Ryan, 2026-09-28): after a bout
  both integrations are running, so a re-do could not start cold. To race again, the presenter
  prepares a new bout, which meets both lanes parked.
- **Not timed:** anything the installer builds. Each integration's start from parked **is** timed, on
  both lanes; that is what cold at the bell means.
- **Frozen before the first scored bout, in the protocol constants:**
  - poll periods;
  - verifier SQL;
  - the competitor's configuration;
  - the launch-skew bound;
  - every timeout.

  Spikes set timeouts and chaos delays. They never choose the metric.

### 2. Parked at the bell

Decided for Round 4 on 2026-09-28: both lanes are parked when the bell rings, and each clock contains
its own integration's start. This adds no Round 5-style coordinator: each lane has one idempotent
`start` and one idempotent `park` on standing resources, provider state is the only truth, and
nothing is created per bout.

**Round 6 differs on one side, and says so (Ryan, 2026-09-29).** Lakebase's lane is its built-in
change feed, a `CdfConfig` on the source database. Its API offers only create and delete, no start
or stop, and it streams continuously: a feed reporting `CDF_STATE_STREAMING` held through 24× its
endpoint's suspend window. So there is nothing on Lakebase's side to park. Of the three ways to
treat that, Ryan chose the first:

1. **AWS cold at the bell, Lakebase's feed always on (chosen).** The AWS lane's DMS task and Glue
   job are parked between bouts and started at the bell, as he asked for Rounds 4 and 6 ("it should
   always be cold for AWS"). The card names the condition, "Lakebase: built-in change feed · AWS: DMS
   + Glue (cold start)", and What It Cost states what keeping AWS's pipeline running all day would
   cost, the fair comparison to a feed that is part of the database.
2. Both always on: a warm comparison, which the project rules out everywhere.
3. Both cold, by deleting and recreating Lakebase's feed per bout: that times building the
   integration rather than moving data, and it is fragile.

1. **Park between bouts.** After a bout the source row is restored, both running lanes carry the
   restore to their destinations, and then both integrations are parked. A towel parks them, and so
   does a failed lane, because a lane that is not healthy is not left running.
2. **Prepare starts nothing.** It confirms both lanes are parked, waiting out a park still in
   progress, and that the baseline is exact on every destination. PR #41's warm keeper, which warms
   Round 4's pipeline while the app is in use, would put one lane ahead at the bell, so v1.1 retires
   it for Round 4 (§ 11). The one exception is repair: if a crash left a destination off its
   baseline, Prepare first runs the same restore-carry-park sequence, visibly, before it confirms.
3. **The bell starts both lanes at once** and commits the change. The two start calls and the commit
   are issued together; neither lane's start waits for the other.
4. **A start is refused only for a reason the provider states.** AWS Glue reports a stopped run as
   `STOPPED` before it releases the job's run slot: in the cold race, a start issued seconds after a
   stop was refused with `ConcurrentRunsExceededException`. So a park is not complete until that
   release has settled, and the bell's start retries that one error, within a bound. The job's
   concurrent-run limit stays at one: two runs would share one streaming checkpoint.
5. **Round 6's AWS lane parks two things.** Its DMS task is stopped between bouts (its replication
   instance stands, and bills, whether or not the task runs) and its Glue job has no run. The bell
   resumes the task from its standing slot and starts the job, which resumes from its own
   checkpoint, so each reads exactly the changes since it last stopped.

### 3. Fairness against Lakebase

1. **Same change, same verifier, same poll period.**
   - Round 4 uses literally one commit.
   - Round 6 commits one checkout to both sources, with the skew bounded.
   - Each round uses one verifier for both lanes: the same SQL, client and TLS mode
     (`sslmode=require`, as every round's database connection on main), with each lane's own
     application credential. The poll period is v1's: 250 ms for Round 4's fresh read, 1 s for
     Round 6's aggregate. The controller stamps every poll.
   - **Nothing else waits on a verifier's path.** Each lane's status watch and every progress
     report run on their own tasks. The first built bouts asked each integration for its status
     inline, and Lakebase's pipeline status, two API reads of about 1.3 s, opened 1.6 s holes in
     Lakebase's reads while the AWS lane was read every 250 ms.
   - **The verifier reads the destination table itself, on both lanes.** A lane's clock never waits
     on a status API. Only Lakebase has one: the synced table's sync-end timestamp. The AWS writer
     has nothing comparable, and a sync-status poll in front of the read would put the app's own
     polling on one clock and not the other (see the two clocks under
     [Measured again after PR #41](#measured-again-after-pr-41-main-bbadc98-2026-09-28)). v1's
     exact-version and timestamp checks stay, as guardrails run after the race, never on the
     clock. Reverse ETL sync stays on the card as a Lakebase-only diagnostic, labeled as the
     service's own figure and never compared across lanes.
2. **Cold at the bell, started the same way.** The bell starts every parked integration at once, so
   a parked lane's clock contains its own start from parked, and only that. In Round 6 only AWS's
   lane is parkable (§ 2), and the card says so.
   - Right before the bell, each lane's destination (Round 4) or source (Round 6) is woken with a
     verified read, and the SQL warehouse is confirmed running. v1 already wakes Lakebase this way.
   - So no clock contains a Lakebase resume, an Aurora resume or a warehouse start: only the
     integration's own start and the change it carries.
3. **Independent lanes.** One bell, no barrier, no lockstep polling. A lane stops the moment its own
   read verifies.
4. **The competitor's best documented configuration, never a strawman.**
   - Continuous modes where the product offers them.
   - In Round 4, AWS moves the data: an AWS Glue streaming job, Spark on AWS compute, at a
     one-second trigger. A Databricks pipeline writing into Aurora would be Databricks moving it, and
     AWS DMS cannot read a lakehouse at all: its sources include no Databricks, Delta, Iceberg,
     Hudi, Redshift or Athena, and its S3 source reads only CSV, or Parquet that DMS itself wrote.
   - In Round 6, AWS moves the data too, into the lakehouse in an open table format (Ryan: "mine is
     'Lakehouse' not redshift"). AWS DMS change capture reads the r6 database's logical replication,
     with S3 Parquet output at DMS's shortest batch settings (`CdcMaxBatchInterval` 1 s,
     `CdcMinFileSize` 1 KB). An AWS Glue 5.0 streaming job appends each change to a Delta table at a
     one-second trigger, which the lakehouse reads as a Unity Catalog external table. The other AWS
     routes, checked 2026-09-29:
     - Aurora and RDS zero-ETL land only in Redshift, and Glue zero-ETL takes no Aurora or RDS
       source.
     - DMS on its own writes files, not a table.
     - Amazon Data Firehose's database source into Iceberg is still in preview ("Amazon Data
       Firehose is in preview release and is subject to change", its API reference), and needs a
       PrivateLink service in front of the database.
   - All of it chosen by the spikes and frozen before scoring.
5. **Matched capacity and region.** The r4 and r6 databases get the same 4 GB ceiling as every other
   round: Aurora 0–2 ACU, RDS `db.t4g.medium`, Lakebase 0.5–2 CU. `capacity_parity` extends to rounds
   4 and 6. One region for every timed endpoint and compute plane; arm refuses a mismatch.
6. **The setup asymmetry is shown, measured, never hidden.** This matches how Round 5 lists its
   install-time prerequisites outside its clock.
   - **Components per lane.**
     - Lakebase: one synced table, or one built-in change feed.
     - AWS, Round 4: a Glue streaming job per competitor and their script, an IAM role that reads
       the table's files from S3 (around Unity Catalog), an isolated subnet with its own route table
       and S3 endpoint, a Glue security group and a database ingress rule, a Glue connection per
       competitor with its writer role, a target table with its ledger, and a checkpoint per run.
     - AWS, Round 6: logical replication on the r6 database, a DMS replication instance with its
       subnet group, a source and an S3 target endpoint, a CDC task and its standing replication
       slot, an S3 bucket, a Glue streaming job with its role and checkpoint, and a Unity Catalog
       storage credential, external location and external table.
   - **A parked lane's start is inside its clock** (§ 2), and the card names the condition: "both
     integrations cold start at the bell" in Round 4, and in Round 6 "Lakebase: built-in change feed
     · AWS: DMS + Glue (cold start)".
   - **Standing cost in What It Cost,** including that Aurora with logical replication never pauses,
     and, in Round 6, what keeping AWS's pipeline running all day would cost.
7. **Resolution from evidence, and one-sided outcomes never win.**
   - For each lane, **U** is when its first exact read completed. **L** is the later of its commit's
     acknowledgment and the start of its last negative read.
   - A lane wins only if all of these hold:
     - both lanes verified;
     - Round 6's skew is within bound;
     - its interval ends before the other's begins (U_A ≤ L_B).
   - The margin is U_B − U_A. Overlap reads `WITHIN MEASUREMENT RESOLUTION`.
   - Each lane's largest gap between reads is recorded with its result and gates nothing. A slow
     read only widens that lane's interval, which can turn a win into `WITHIN MEASUREMENT
     RESOLUTION` and never the reverse. Measured on the built engine: 251–365 ms on both lanes.
   - A towel, or one-sided completion, is a censored lower bound, in Round 3's receipt shape.
   - This replaces the branch's resolution, which paired polls across lanes and could not award a win
     to a lane whose first read was already positive.

### 4. The lanes

#### Round 4: lakehouse data into a live application

| | Lakebase lane | AWS lane (Aurora or RDS) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Standing (installer)** | The v1 synced table (CONTINUOUS) from the shared Delta source into the r4 Lakebase database, its pipeline parked | One AWS Glue streaming job per competitor, parked, reading the shared source from S3. Each writes a standing target table, with its high-water/tombstone ledger, in the r4 Aurora cluster or RDS instance. |
| **Prepare** | Confirm the pipeline is parked and the baseline exact; start nothing | Confirm the job is parked (its run slot released, § 2) and the baseline exact; start nothing |
| **Bell** | Start the pipeline, and the shared scored `MERGE`: score, model version, per-bout nonce | Start the job run, and the same commit |
| **Clock stops** | First fresh application read that returns the exact tuple | The same read against Aurora or RDS |
| **After** | The source row is restored | The same restore reaches its target |
| **Park** | Stop the pipeline | Stop the job run, then wait for Glue to release its run slot |

- **Writer configuration.** AWS moves the data: an AWS Glue 5.0 Spark job (`glueetl`, two G.1X
  workers) running a Structured Streaming query at a one-second trigger. It reads the Delta table straight from its S3 location with
  Glue's open-source Delta reader, and writes through the JDBC driver Glue ships, over a Glue
  connection per competitor that also holds its credentials. Spike S1 froze this configuration;
  nothing in it is tuned for or against either lane.
- **One job per competitor.** Two jobs, one script. A bout starts only its own competitor's job, so a
  stuck Aurora run can never hold up an RDS bout, and neither job has a second connection to set up.
- **Every run gets a fresh checkpoint, and a bout's run resumes.** No run depends on an earlier
  run's checkpoint, or on change files older than itself, which Delta's vacuum is free to remove.
  At the bell the app tells the run the version just after the one Prepare verified every
  destination holds. The run reads the change feed from there, which is what a checkpointed stream
  resuming would read and what Lakebase's pipeline reads, so its first batch is the bout's own
  change. The run waits for that commit if it gets there first. A run that settles or proves the
  lane, where nothing is timed, opens with the table's current snapshot instead.
  - This replaced opening every run with the snapshot. On the test installation (2026-09-29), that
    made the first batch's `latestOffset` take 20.8 s and 29.2 s. Most of it was one Spark job
    listing the snapshot's files from 41–43 log entries (12.8 s and 21.0 s), which Lakebase's
    resuming pipeline never runs.
- **Credentials.** Each r4 database has a writer role, `LOGIN NOSUPERUSER NOREPLICATION`, owning its
  schema and nothing else. The installer sets its password and puts the same password on the Glue
  connection, and sets both again on every re-run. The master password is never used: RDS rotates it
  every seven days, which would break the connection silently. No Secrets Manager secret is needed,
  because nothing but the Glue connection ever reads the writer's password.
- **Backstop.** Each job carries a 30-minute run timeout. A run that nothing stops, because the app
  died, stops itself within half an hour, at a cost of about $0.44.
- **Writer correctness.** The writer reduces the change feed by the rules in
  [Round 4 CDF change reduction](#round-4-cdf-change-reduction) below, kept from the branch's
  `competitive-rounds-v2.md`. Its prototype broke one of them (it kept the first of two differing
  same-type images), so every rule gets its own test.

#### Round 4 CDF change reduction

The writer consumes the Delta change data feed. Each change row carries a `_change_type` of
`insert`, `update_preimage`, `update_postimage` or `delete`, plus `_commit_version` and
`_commit_timestamp`. Reduction to the exact destination state is deterministic, never best-effort
or arrival-ordered.

- **Pre-images are never applied.** Only `insert` and `update_postimage` rows contribute an upsert
  image. Applying an `update_preimage` is a defect, not a tie to break.
- **Cross-type resolution at one version.** When one key has *different* change types at the same
  `_commit_version`, a fixed precedence decides: `delete` outranks `update_postimage`, which
  outranks `insert`. It applies only across different types and never picks between two rows of
  the same type.
- **Same-type rows with differing images are unorderable.** For a given
  `(key, _commit_version, _change_type)`, every row must carry the same image; one commit yields at
  most one net change per key, so this normally holds. If the images differ, the change feed exposes
  no order within the commit, so the reducer must not pick one. It marks the key unresolved and
  applies the authoritative Delta snapshot of that key as of that `_commit_version`. If it cannot,
  the batch fails. No order is ever invented.
- **Delete precedence.** If the resolved change at a key's highest version is a `delete`, the key is
  deleted, whatever lower versions held.
- **A transactional high-water and tombstone ledger.** Per key, the ledger records the highest
  applied `_commit_version`, and a tombstone when that version's change was a delete, so a replayed
  lower-version image cannot resurrect the key. The ledger update and the target's upsert or delete
  commit in the same database transaction. The simplest form keeps the ledger on the target row
  itself (a version column and a `deleted` flag, a delete being a tombstone rather than a physical
  delete), so one guarded `INSERT … ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE … WHERE version < excluded.version` is
  both; the application read then asks for `NOT deleted`. The branch prototype used that form.
- **A monotonic guard read from the ledger.** A change is applied only when its `_commit_version`
  is strictly greater than the ledger's high-water mark for that key; the row's presence is never
  consulted. That makes a duplicate a no-op, and a late or replayed lower version, discarded.
- **The ledger belongs to one Delta table.** Each row also records the source's Delta table ID, and a
  change from a different table ID always applies. Round 4's source repair (`CREATE OR REPLACE` when
  its Delta path is missing) restarts the versions at zero, and a guard on versions alone would then
  refuse every change forever.

#### Round 6: live application data into the lakehouse

| | Lakebase lane | AWS lane (Aurora or RDS) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Standing (installer)** | The v1 CDF config on its source table, writing its Delta history table | Per competitor: <br>• the r6 database with its logical-replication parameter group attached from the start <br>• the source table <br>• a DMS CDC task on the installation's replication instance, with its source and S3 target endpoints, and its standing slot <br>• a Glue streaming job and its checkpoint <br>• the Delta history table in the lane's bucket, a Unity Catalog external table |
| **Prepare** | Confirm the feed is `CDF_STATE_STREAMING` and the baseline exact, in the source and in history | Confirm the task is stopped and the job has no run (its run slot released), and the baseline exact, in the source and in history; start nothing |
| **Bell** | Commit the $84.50 checkout with its nonce | Commit the same checkout, concurrently, and start the DMS task and the Glue job |
| **Clock stops** | First read of its Delta history table, on the sealed SQL warehouse, that returns the exact insert | The same read of its Delta history table |
| **Guardrail** | A separate checkout commits and reads back on Lakebase while the history is awaited (v1) | The same, on Aurora or RDS |
| **After** | Delete the checkout rows (v1) | Delete the checkout rows. The delete is captured at the next bell; per-bout nonces keep an old row from ever matching. |
| **Park** | Nothing to stop: the feed is built in (§ 2) | Stop the DMS task and the Glue run, then wait for Glue to release its run slot |

- **Verifier.** The same SQL on both lanes, on the same sealed SQL warehouse: that bout's order ID
  and nonce, as one insert, in the lane's Delta history table. Both are change histories, one row per
  change: Lakebase's feed writes a change type and LSN, and DMS writes its operation and commit
  timestamp. v1's one-second poll period applies to both, each lane on its own task (§ 3).
- **Representation is disclosed, not equalized.** The claim is delivery of that row. This guardrail is
  kept from the branch's `receipt-and-claims.md` (its claim guardrails).
- **The slot is standing.** DMS creates its replication slot at the task's first start, at install,
  and a stopped task keeps it. A standing slot retains WAL while the task is stopped: an idle RDS
  source adds 768 MB an hour (measured, below). So the parameter group sets a finite
  `max_slot_wal_keep_size`. Prepare moves the parked slot up to the current WAL position, so the
  bell's resume reads only what the bout writes, however long the installation sat idle. A slot
  PostgreSQL dropped is re-created there too, never inside the clock. Doctor does not yet report
  the slot's retained WAL.
- **Measured cold, 2026-09-29** (spike, below): the exact row in the lakehouse at 62–93 s. DMS wrote
  its change file 7–16 s after the commit, starting cold. Glue's cold start is nearly all the rest.

Four paths: Round 4 × {Aurora, RDS} and Round 6 × {Aurora, RDS}. The installer builds everything

Round 4's AWS lane is built in a second, additive Terraform apply, after Round 4's Delta source
exists, because the Glue role is read-only on that table's one S3 prefix and the prefix is known only
once the table is. Three things about it are checked at install rather than assumed:

- **The source storage must be readable from the installation's AWS account.** The Glue role reads
  the managed table's files directly, which works when the workspace's storage bucket is in that
  account and encrypted with S3-managed keys. The installer's first run of each job is the proof,
  and its failure names the refusal.
- **The Glue subnet's CIDR** is a /24 derived from the installation ID inside the VPC, above the
  default subnets, so two installations in one VPC do not collide. An explicit VPC names it.
- **The lane is proven end to end at install.** Each job runs once, loads the table's snapshot into
  its target, and is parked, so a broken role, route or credential fails the install instead of the
  first bout.

Round 6's AWS lane has the same shape: a second, additive apply after Round 6's Lakebase side is
sealed, and a first run of each job at install that carries the baseline into its Delta history table,
so a broken role, route, endpoint or grant fails the install instead of the first bout.

Five install-time risks get explicit handling.

- **DMS's account-wide service roles.** DMS in a VPC requires `dms-vpc-role`, and task logging
  `dms-cloudwatch-logs-role`. Both names are fixed by AWS for the whole account. The installer adopts
  either role if it exists and never deletes one it did not create, or a second installation in the
  account would lose it (the collision class of the fixes before PR #27).
- **Unity Catalog privileges.** The storage credential and external location need privileges that
  only a metastore admin normally holds. The installer seals `eligible` or `not eligible`, with the
  exact refusal. Without them, Round 6's AWS lane shows `NOT SUPPORTED · N/A` and the reason, and the
  Lakebase lane runs alone, as in v1.
- **The standing slot's WAL** (§ 4): a finite `max_slot_wal_keep_size`, retained WAL in `doctor`,
  and an invalidated slot repaired by bring-up.
- **TLS.** v1.1 is built from main, which connects with `sslmode=require` everywhere, and Round
  4's AWS lane does the same. The branch's sealed `verify-full` CA (commit `551063b`, which makes
  every Postgres connector require `*_CA_BUNDLE`/`*_CA_SHA256` that nothing sets) is not part of
  v1.1. Adopting it would be its own change, across every round at once.
- **Stable Terraform addresses.** The live installation already holds the branch's r4/r6 addresses.
  v1.1 keeps them, and its plan against that state must be additive only: no replace, no destroy.
- **Lease and seals.**
  - Every new tagged resource gets `ignore_changes = [tags["expires-at"]]`. Parameter groups stay
    attached so the lease finds them.
  - The AWS-lane seals get their own manifest sections, `round4_aws` and `round6_aws`, and never live
    inside `Round4Resources`. On the branch, any change there wipes the Round 5 and Round 6 seals.
  - Round 4 and Round 6 must stay sealable without their AWS outputs.

The hard-coded round lists that must learn 4 and 6 (found on main): `infra/aws/locals.tf`;
`_V7_ROUND_KEYS`, `_V7_RDS_ROUND_KEYS` and `_v7_aws_environment_seals` in `server/lifecycle.py`;
`_capacity_parity`, `_aws_ingress`, `_rds_ingress` and `_ownership_environments`;
`server/aws_permissions.py`; and `server/cost_model.py`.

### 6. Cleanup, restart and uninstall

- **Per bout, only rows.** Round 4's source row is restored as soon as the result is in, while both
  lanes still run to carry it. Round 6's checkout rows are deleted.
- **`park`, per lane, is idempotent and name-scoped.**
  - Round 4 Lakebase: stop the pipeline, waiting out `STOPPING`.
  - Round 4 AWS: stop the job's run, wait for `STOPPED`, then wait for Glue to release the run slot
    (§ 2).
  - Round 6 AWS: stop the DMS task and wait for `stopped`; stop the Glue run, wait for `STOPPED`,
    then wait for Glue to release the run slot. The replication slot stays, because it is standing
    (§ 4).
  - Round 6 Lakebase: nothing; its feed is built in.
- **Who calls `park`:**
  - the end of every bout;
  - a towel, and a failed lane;
  - **Retry cleanup**;
  - Prepare, whenever a lane is not parked or a destination is off its baseline;
  - uninstall.
- **Cleanup owed is observable.** The owed state is simply the provider state, found by name: a
  running pipeline, DMS task or Glue job while no bout is in flight, or leftover rows.
  The card offers only **Retry cleanup**. Built so far for Round 4: Lakebase's owed stop is on
  `/readyz`, through main's `owed_stop_notice`. A Glue run left running is not reported there yet;
  its 30-minute timeout bounds it, and the next Prepare parks it.
- **Restart mid-bout.**
  - The session is gone, as in every other round, and no score is ever reconstructed.
  - Round 4 runs nothing at startup. The next Prepare parks both lanes, waiting out a stop in
    progress and Glue's slot release, puts the row back and arms, visibly. A bout killed 15 s after
    its bell was healed this way in 82 s (§ 10).
  - Until somebody prepares, what a crash costs is bounded twice over. Lakebase's stop, owed from
    the bell, is repaid 30 minutes later by the startup recovery main already has. The Glue run
    stops itself at its 30-minute timeout, at about $0.44.
  - Graceful shutdown mid-bout still reports Round 4 clean without parking it
    (`_cleanup_for_shutdown`). Settling takes longer than its 30-second budget, and a failed
    cleanup would hold the ring lease until it expires. Having it ask both lanes to stop, without
    waiting, would bring billing down to about a minute; that is the one piece of this not built.
- **Uninstall.**
  1. Park every lane.
  2. Delete the standing Databricks objects: pipelines, Round 6's external tables, external location
     and storage credential, secret-scope keys, schemas and volumes.
  3. Terraform destroys the rest, the r6 databases with their slots among it. DMS's account-wide
     service roles are deleted only if this installation created them (§ 5).
  - Cleanup refuses while any lane cannot be proven parked.

There is no attempt journal, because nothing per bout needs one.

### 7. Cost

    `IMPUTED_AURORA_ROUNDS`). v1.1 moves them to billed, so they are not counted twice.

  - Round 4's synced-table pipeline: about $0.60 per hour, main's own figure;

### 11. Decisions

Decided by Ryan on 2026-09-28:

- **AWS moves the data.** Round 4's AWS lane is an AWS Glue streaming job, not a Databricks pipeline
  writing into Aurora, and DMS is out because it cannot read a lakehouse.
- **Both lanes are cold at the bell.** Each clock contains its own integration's start, and a
  comparison with one lane warm or both warm appears nowhere in the project. On 2026-09-29 Ryan asked
  for it to be said in the room: each lane is labeled "(cold start)", and the card says "both
  integrations cold start at the bell" (`2b89bd2`).
- **Prepare must not make the room wait** (Ryan, 2026-09-29). Round 4's databases stay awake while the
  app is on screen, and Prepare overlaps its own waits, so it took 3.9–6.6 s where a paused Aurora made
  it 23 s (`fe4b73a`, `545daf1`). The integrations stay parked, so the race stays cold.
- **The verdict fits on one line,** as in Rounds 1 to 3: "LAKEBASE WINS · MARGIN 62.8s" (`2b89bd2`).
- **The implementation base** is a fresh `v1.1` branch from main that ports only reviewed *Keep*
  and *Rework* items.
- **Step 3 shipped early,** as PR #41.
- **A separate test installation** carries the spikes and the install gate.
- **Round 4 races both Aurora and RDS.** Its standing cost is the r4 databases; Round 4's Aurora
  cluster can still pause, because it needs no logical replication.
- **Round 4 has no re-do.** After a bout both integrations are running, so a re-do could not start
  cold. Racing again is a new bout.
- **PR #41's warm keeper stops warming Round 4.** It would put Lakebase ahead at the bell, which the
  both-cold rule forbids, so Round 4's Prepare only confirms both lanes parked.

Decided by Ryan on 2026-09-29, for Round 6:

## Not doing: the complexity budget

- No backstage warm coordinator, no hour-long warm, no runner fleet. Each lane has one idempotent
  `start`, issued at the bell, and one idempotent `park`, issued after the bout.
- No per-bout creation of integrations, and no short-lived objects: Round 6's DMS slot stands with its
  task.
- No attempt journal and no adoption protocol. Deterministic names plus provider state are the
  ledger.
- No second lease. The existing ring lease is the only mutual exclusion.
- No HMAC receipts, promotion ledger, anti-rollback anchor or schema validator.
- No verdict computed in the browser.
- No DMS in Round 4, which reads a lakehouse and DMS cannot. Round 6's DMS lane writes into one.

Anything added later has to argue its way past this list.
